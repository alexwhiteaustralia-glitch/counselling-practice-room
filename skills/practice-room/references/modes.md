# Modes

Step-by-step instructions for each mode. Read the section for the mode the student starts. The client rules, nudges, debrief and session note live in `SKILL.md`.

## Teach ("teach me [skill]")

1. Explain the card in plain speech: the skill summary, how to set up safety for it, the core moves, and the main things to avoid. Under about 90 seconds.
2. Ask one of the card's "Think about" questions, aimed at the student's own practice.
3. Respond to their answer briefly, correcting or extending.
4. Offer the next step: "Want to hear it done, or go straight to reps?"

## Arrive ("arrive", or "ready" with Arrive on)

A pre-session ritual drawn from Geller's work on therapeutic presence. Under 60 seconds, slow, with a short pause after each line:

"Pause. Let what you were doing stop. Relax, let the shoulders and jaw soften. Expand your awareness to the room. Sense your body and what you're feeling. Engage with both your inside and what's around you. Notice the space where the client will be. Centre yourself. And enter with openness."

Then: "Open whenever you're ready," and start the session they set up. Offer Arrive before every Rehearsal, Deep session, Replay and casebook session, and before a Session if the student sounds rushed. Don't offer it before drills.

## Warm-up ("warm up")

Mixed reps to shake off the rust before a session or recording.

1. Give three to five single client lines, one to three sentences each, each suited to a different skill (a ramble with several threads suits a summary, a charged word suits a restatement, a feeling in passing suits an empathic reflection).
2. The student responds with whatever they think fits.
3. Step out of role: verdict ("Landed", "Close" or "Missed"), which skill would have served best, and one reason. Invite a second try if close or missed.
4. Across the set, include at least one "I feel that..." line (a thought with an unnamed feeling underneath), one client escaping into story, and one moment where something audible shifts (a pause, a laugh, a voice dropping) that invites an observation.
5. Afterwards, name the pattern across the reps in two sentences, including how many responses were questions, and offer to carry that one note into a Rehearsal.

## Edge drill ("edge drill", "edge drill [edge]")

Practises the student's own habits rather than a single skill. Every rep is built so the habitual move is the tempting one, and the student practises not taking it.

1. **Find the edges.** Read the Running edges in `Practice_Log.md` and take the ones marked as edges or still live, up to three, favouring any marked still live in recent edge checks. If the student names one ("edge drill reassurance"), use only that. If there's no log, no edges, or no file tools, ask in one sentence which habit they want to work on, offering the common ones: a tack-on question after a good reflection or close, reassurance or praise when the client shrinks, filling a silence, stepping back from heat, asking where a statement would do, and falling back to content when the feeling word won't come.
2. **Say what's in play.** One sentence naming the edges. Then start. Don't say which edge a given rep targets.
3. **Six reps.** Each rep, set the moment in one sentence, then give a client line in role built to bait one edge. Rotate so each edge gets at least two reps. Bait patterns:
   - **Tack-on:** the client lands something real and goes quiet. The move is a short reflection or their phrase handed back, then stop.
   - **Reassurance or praise:** the client apologises or shrinks ("sorry, this is probably nothing"), ideally in a way that mirrors their issue. The move is to reflect the apology or the feeling under it.
   - **Filling the silence:** the client stops mid-thought or trails off ("I dunno..."). The move is to wait, or give a minimal encourager. Claude can't hear how long the student waited, so ask them afterwards and take their word for it.
   - **Stepping back from heat:** the client asks something direct ("so what am I meant to do?") or reaches the strongest feeling of the rep. The move is to meet that first, without switching to the body, a new topic, a principle or self-disclosure.
   - **Question over statement:** the client half-names a feeling where a reflection fits. The move is a statement with no question mark.
   - **Feeling vocabulary:** the client describes a situation at medium intensity without naming the feeling. The move is one specific feeling word at the right intensity, not a recap.
   - **Any other edge in the log:** build the moment where that habit usually shows up, using the edge's own description.
4. The student responds, then stops.
5. **Student calls it first.** Ask "Held or slipped?" and take the answer. Then step out of role: verdict ("Held", "Close" or "Slipped"), naming the edge and one reason. Play the client's next line so the student hears what the response did. A slip gets the reaction the client rules describe; a hold lets the client go a little further.
6. Invite a second try if close or slipped. Model a line only after the second attempt.
7. **Rep 6 doubles up:** two baits at once, such as an apology followed by trailing off, or a direct question at peak feeling.
8. **Close.** For each edge, one sentence: held on how many of its reps, and whether the student's own call matched the verdict. If the last session note has an edge check, compare in a phrase. One carry forward.

**Logging.** The session note's edge check uses the drill's counts: an edge is shrinking only if it held on most of its reps, and steady or still live otherwise. When an edge has held cleanly in two edge drills and the next two session edge checks, ask whether to move it to a strength.

**Tone.** These are the student's own habits, so keep it light and matter of fact. A slip is information, not a verdict on them. If the student pushes back on an edge ("I don't think I do that any more"), offer to run two reps on it and let the result answer.

## Question bank ("question bank [phase]" or "question bank")

Practises choosing and adapting a line from `references/question-bank.md` to fit the moment, instead of reciting it.

1. If no phase is given, ask which: opening, goal setting, exploring, deepening, ambivalence and change, ending, or mixed.
2. Set the moment in one sentence, then give a client line in role.
3. The student responds, using a bank line as written, adapted, or in their own words.
4. Step out of role: verdict, whether the line suited the moment and the phase, and one reason. If the line belongs to a higher level than the student is up to, say so lightly and suggest an alternative.
5. Every second round, flip it: give the moment and the client line, then offer two bank lines and ask which one fits and why the other doesn't.
6. Five rounds, then the pattern in two sentences and one carry forward.

"Remind me [phase]" gives three lines from that phase in about 20 seconds, with one note on timing.

## Spot it ("spot it [skill]")

1. Give one client line, in the client's voice.
2. Give two counsellor responses, "Response one" and "Response two". Base one on an item from the card's Avoid list, and make the other solid. Vary which comes first.
3. Ask: "Which lands better, and what's the difference?"
4. Let them answer fully, then confirm or sharpen it using the card's language.
5. Up to three rounds, then offer the drill.

## Drill ("drill [skill]")

Five reps that get harder:

- Reps 1 and 2: clear, one main point.
- Reps 3 and 4: content mixed with feeling, or two threads.
- Rep 5: one of the card's "If the client drifts" or "Notice in the client" situations, such as going off track, correcting the counsellor, going flat, or saying something loaded in passing.

For each rep:

1. Say the client line in role, one to three sentences.
2. The student responds.
3. Step out of role and give a verdict ("Landed", "Close" or "Missed") with one reason, tied to the card's observer checks.
4. If it was close or missed, invite a second try. Only offer a model line after their second attempt.
5. If the student wants to re-run a rep, repeat the client line word for word.
6. If the student produces a useful phrase or asks to keep one, say "noted for the log" and include it in the session note. If they want it on their shortlist, offer "add favourite".

After rep five, describe the pattern across the reps in two sentences, then give one carry forward. Offer the next step.

"Drill all skills" runs as a Warm-up. "Drill working with ambivalence" runs as the Change talk drill.

## Ladder drill ("ladder drill")

Climbs the microskills hierarchy on a single client line.

1. Give one client line in role, two to four sentences, with something observable, some content, a feeling, a small discrepancy, more than one thread, and a hint of what it means to them.
2. Ask for one response per rung, one at a time, bottom up: observe, encourage or paraphrase, one open question, reflect the feeling, name a discrepancy (skip if the student isn't up to Level 3), focus, reflect the meaning.
3. After each rung: verdict and one reason, checked against that rung's card. Invite a second try if close or missed.
4. At the top, name the rung that came easiest and the one that took most work, in two sentences, and one carry forward.
5. Variation: "ladder drill, top down" starts at meaning and works down.

Repeat the client line word for word before each rung.

## Stem drill ("stem drill")

Builds a ready range of reflection openers. Uses the Reflection stems card.

1. Each rep, name a stem ("That sounds like...", "I can see...", "It's as if...", "I wonder if...") and give a client line in role.
2. The student finishes the stem for that client, then stops.
3. Verdict and one reason. Check that the stem fits the moment and, most of all, that the word after it is specific. A good stem with a vague ending ("that sounds like a strong experience") is a Close at best.
4. Five reps, mixing the three stem families. In the last rep, let the student choose the stem.
5. Pattern in two sentences: which stem they reach for by default, and which family they rarely use.

## Favourites ("favourites", "favourites drill", "add favourite", "build favourites")

Each student keeps their own shortlist in `Favourite_Skills.md`: the stems, questions and frames they most want to practise, grouped under short headings. The skill holds the rules; the list is theirs.

**"build favourites"** (text chat with file tools). Offer a checklist built from the skill cards and question bank: the reflection stems, the open and phenomenological questions, the confronting and immediacy lines, the ambivalence and change lines, the check-outs, the silence and close lines, and a handful of question bank lines from each phase. Group them under the card names, a few words each. If the chat can show an interactive checklist, use one; otherwise give a numbered list and ask the student to reply with numbers. Also ask: "Any lines of your own to add, from other training, supervision or your own practice?" Then write `Favourite_Skills.md` with the ticked lines under their group headings, the student's own lines under "My own", and a "Frames" heading for anything that's a stance or something to track rather than a line to say. Add the date. Confirm in one line.

**"add favourite [line]"**. Read `Favourite_Skills.md`, add the line under the best-fitting heading (or "My own"), write it back, and confirm in one line. If the line is a reassurance, a "why" question, a leading question, or anything else on a card's Avoid list, say so in one sentence and ask whether they still want it. It's their list. If there are no file tools, give the line ready to paste and say it isn't saved yet.

**"favourites"**. Read the list back: headings and a count in voice, the full list in text. Then offer the drill.

**"favourites drill"**. Reps drawn from the student's own list. If `Favourite_Skills.md` doesn't exist, say so and offer "build favourites", or a stem drill in the meantime.

1. Each rep, pick one line from the list, rotating headings and not repeating a line until the list has been covered. In one short sentence, set where the session is up to so the line fits. Then give a client line in role, built so that favourite is what the moment calls for.
2. For the first three reps, don't name the favourite: the student has to recognise which one fits. After that, name it first if they ask for "named reps".
3. The student responds, then stops.
4. Step out of role: verdict and one reason, checked against the card the line comes from. Always check: the right favourite for the moment, the word after a stem specific, a statement where a statement was called for, and no tack-on after it.
5. Play the client's next line so the student hears whether it opened something. Invite a second try if close or missed. Model a line only after the second attempt.
6. **Frames.** If the list has a Frames heading, once per drill, ask one out-of-role question about one frame: where the client seemed to be in it, or what the frame would have the student notice. Frames are never scored as lines.
7. Six reps, then the pattern in two sentences: which favourites are already automatic and which still sound like a script. One carry forward.

**Scope.** Favourites from other modalities, coaching or body-based methods are fine to drill, but the client still follows the client rules: simple sensations only, nothing worked actively. If a favourite would steer the client before their feeling has been met, flag it lightly.

**Retiring.** In the session note, list the favourites that landed clean. When one has landed clean in three favourites drills, ask whether to move it to a "Settled" heading at the bottom of the list.

## Unpack drill ("unpack drill")

Walks a five-step unpacking sequence on one client moment. Uses the Unpacking and triggers card.

1. In role, the client mentions a specific moment that stirred them, compressed into a summary ("it was just a bad day", "I snapped at my brother, whatever").
2. The student takes the client through the steps, one response at a time: presenting moment, sensory and body awareness, emotional awareness, associative meaning, integration. The client answers each in role, revealing a little more when the step is done well.
3. After each step, step out briefly: verdict and one reason. Check pace, order (body and feeling before meaning) and that the client led.
4. **Sequencing check.** If the student asks an origin or pattern question ("when have you felt this before?") before the present feeling has been met, flag it at once. The client answers from their head, briefly and a little flat.
5. Close with the pattern in two sentences and one carry forward. Keep moments mild and everyday.

## Change talk drill ("change talk drill", "short change talk drill")

Practises motivational interviewing responses. Uses the Working with ambivalence card. Eight reps; "short change talk drill" runs reps 1 to 5 only. Use the same client and the same change across all reps, so the student hears how each response shifts the client's talk.

1. Each rep, the client gives two to three sentences about a change they're circling, mixing change talk (desire, ability, reasons, need) and sustain talk.
2. The student responds in one or two sentences.
3. Step out of role: verdict and one reason. Check whether they reflected the change talk, left the sustain talk unargued, and joined two sides with "and" in any double-sided reflection.
4. Reps 4 and 5 bait the righting reflex: the client says something that invites advice or a case for change ("everyone says I should just quit"). Arguing for change is a Missed, and the client's next line hardens into sustain talk.
5. **Rep 6, ruler.** The student asks an importance or confidence ruler in their own words. The client gives a middle number. The follow-up is the skill. "What makes it a 5 and not a 2?" draws out change talk. "Why not a 9?" or "What's stopping you?" draws out sustain talk: that's a Missed. For confidence, "What would get you from a 5 to a 6?" is the move. Stopping at the number without a follow-up is a Close.
6. **Rep 7, amplified reflection.** The client gives firm sustain talk ("honestly, it's not a problem"). The student reflects it back slightly overstated, said plainly and without sarcasm ("so there's nothing about it that bothers you at all"). Landed: the client backs off a little and offers some change talk. Sarcasm, or overstating so far it sounds like mockery: the client digs in or goes cool. Not overstating at all gives a simple reflection, which is a Close.
7. **Rep 8, Ask-Offer-Ask.** The client asks for information or advice ("what do other people do?"). The student asks what the client already knows, asks permission to share, offers one short piece of information without telling them what to do, then asks what they make of it. This runs over two or three exchanges. Telling the client what to do, or a lecture of more than two sentences, is a Missed, and the client goes polite and flat.
8. Optional ninth rep: walk the client through one box of a decisional balance, usually the benefits of not changing, and reflect what comes up.
9. Pattern in two sentences and one carry forward. Include who did most of the arguing for change across the drill, the client or the student.

## Culture drill ("culture drill")

Practises staying inside the client's frame when their values or worldview differ from the student's. Uses the Culture and worldview card.

1. Each rep, in role, the client gives two or three sentences where culture shapes what the problem means: family duty, faith, who decides, shame and face, how distress is named, who you'd usually turn to. Vary backgrounds across the reps.
2. The student responds.
3. Step out of role: verdict and one reason. Check whether the response stayed in the client's frame or carried the student's values (independence, putting yourself first, expressing feelings openly, therapy as the obvious help).
4. Rep 3: the obvious reflection from a Western frame misses. If the student imposes it, the client defers politely and goes quieter. If they ask about the client's frame, the client opens up.
5. Rep 4: the student practises one explanatory model question, in their own words, at a moment that fits.
6. Rep 5: something in the room, such as the client asking "are you going to understand this?" or checking whether the counsellor shares their faith. The student practises meeting it honestly without defending or over-reassuring.
7. One out-of-role question: "What did you assume about any of these clients before they told you?" Accept the answer without grading it. Then the pattern in two sentences and one carry forward.

## Close drill ("close drill")

Practises ending well, without the tack-on question. Uses the Ending card.

1. Set the moment in one sentence, then give the client's final line in role.
2. The student gives a close: one or two sentences, then stops.
3. Step out of role: verdict and one reason. Check four things: did it hand back the client's own words or name the shift, did it avoid a question, did it avoid rating the client's performance, and did it open anything new.
4. If close or missed, invite a second try before offering a model.
5. Rotate the client's last line across: a realisation, a decision, a wobble ("is that normal?"), a thank-you, and a guarded client who gives little.
6. Five rounds, then the pattern in two sentences and one carry forward.

If the student asks what a close is for, answer in under 60 seconds: signal the ending gently, consolidate what moved so it doesn't evaporate, and leave the client holding their own insight. A question at the close reopens the work just when it should be settling.

## Repair drill ("repair drill")

Practises rupture and repair. Uses the Rupture and repair card.

1. Set the moment in one sentence, then give a short exchange where the counsellor (voiced by Claude) says something that closes a door: flat advice, a principle delivered coldly, a missed reflection, a move away from what the client asked.
2. The client withdraws in role: goes polite, flatter, or self-deprecating ("maybe I expected too much").
3. The student repairs.
4. Step out of role: verdict and one reason. Then play the client's response so the student hears whether it landed.
5. Three to five rounds.

## Listen drill ("listen drill")

1. In role, the client talks for 60 to 90 seconds about something ordinary, with one main thread, one side detail, and one feeling mentioned only in passing. For the long version, three to four minutes.
2. The student paraphrases or summarises in two or three sentences and checks it back.
3. As observer, check three things: did they catch the main idea, was anything important left out or misread, and did they check for accuracy. Mention the passing feeling if they missed it.
4. Some rounds, the client corrects them ("Not really, it's more that..."), so they can practise the repair: thank them, then try again.

## Drift drill ("drift drill [skill]")

1. A short exchange starts on a clear focus, then the client drifts: changes topic, goes into storytelling, or goes vague.
2. The student re-anchors using that skill's "If the client drifts" move.
3. Give feedback on whether the re-anchoring kept the client's choice intact or took over the direction.

## Session ("session [skill]", or the setup card's start line)

1. Settle anything missing from setup in one question.
2. On "ready", run Arrive if it's on, otherwise give the skill's core move in one sentence, then "Open whenever you're ready."
3. Play the client by the client rules until the student says "end session", then run the debrief.

With **All skills**, there's no single focus. Nudges judge each response against what the moment called for, and the debrief looks at range.

With **Working with ambivalence** as the focus, build the client in contemplation around a change they're genuinely torn about, and give them at least one moment of firm sustain talk, one question asking for information or advice, and one moment where a ruler would fit. Flag a ruler follow-up that draws out sustain talk the same way as a righting reflex.

## Integration session ("integration session")

A session with no single focus skill. The student draws on whatever fits: presence, listening, reflecting, immediacy, attunement, motivational enquiry. In the debrief, the observer notes which skills showed up naturally and where they reached for a technique instead of staying present. Close with two reflection questions: what comes naturally now, and where do you still hesitate?

## Rehearsal ("rehearsal", "practice run")

An integration-style session for a recorded or assessed session coming up. The aim is range within a natural arc.

1. Settle setup, and ask how long the real session runs. Work to that arc, compressed. Offer Arrive first.
2. Build a client with two or three threads on the surface and one thing underneath, so there's room for an opening, encouragers, a paraphrase or restatement, a mid-session summary, empathic reflection, and a close.
3. If the session reaches a deeper moment and nothing has been summarised yet, the client gives one slightly longer stretch of talk that invites a summary.
4. Run the debrief, adding a **range check** to the observer step: which skills appeared, which the session offered room for but didn't get, and whether there was a clear opening, a summary and a close. Give a rough question count against reflections. If the student has shared the criteria they'll be assessed on, tie the gaps to those.

## Deep session ("deep session", "get to know a client")

For staying with one person long enough that the real thing surfaces. Less about range, more about following.

1. Settle setup. Default length 15 to 20 minutes, nudges light (only where something genuinely matters), subject depth moderate or weighty, middle phase. Offer Arrive first.
2. Build a layered client: a surface concern, a second thread that seems separate, and something underneath that only connects the two if the student follows the client rather than the story. Reveal slowly. Where it fits, give the client a polarity (see the Polarities card).
3. Plant, once each and only when the session has earned it: a moment of trust (the client names someone who matters to them, or says something they've never said aloud), and a direct request for help ("so what do I do with that?"). See the "What do I do?" card.
4. No pressure to close early. Let the student choose when to bring it in.
5. In the debrief, the observer focuses on following: where the student tracked the client's thread, where they steered, where they stepped back from heat and what it did to the client.

## Replay ("replay", "re-play", "redo", or a shared transcript)

Re-runs a real practice session from its transcript, so the student can go back to the moment that mattered and try the move they didn't make. Real sessions raise the stakes in ways AI practice can't (a face, tears, an observer, a clock), and the same edges often show up harder there. Replay brings that moment back, a little calmer, with a rewind button.

**What it's for.** Sessions from the student's training where recording was agreed: triads, peer sessions, intensives, recorded assessments. Not real client work outside training, unless the student's course and the client's consent cover it. Replay is a rehearsal for the student, never a portrait of the person: don't use it to analyse, diagnose or speculate about the real client beyond the skills work.

1. **Get the transcript.** Text chat. Pasted or attached. Automatic transcripts often have no speaker labels and garble words: work out who's who from context, and where a line that matters is unclear, ask about that line rather than guessing. When the student's memory differs from the transcript, go with the student: they were in the room. The transcript can include the peer debrief afterwards; keep it apart from the session itself.
2. **Map it, silently.** The client: what they brought, how they talk, the words and phrases that carried weight, and what came up underneath, as far as the transcript shows. The key moments: each place the client handed the counsellor something (a feeling word, an apology, a self-dismissal, a "doesn't matter", a direct request, tears, their pattern showing up live) and what the counsellor did next.
3. **Review first.** If no review has happened in this chat, give a short observer review before replaying: what landed, two to five things to work on (each tied to a quoted moment and a line that would have stayed with the client), and an edge check against the running edges in `Practice_Log.md`. Include anything the peer debrief raised, and say where you agree, disagree or see both sides.
4. **Choose the replay point and targets.** Offer two or three restart moments, each as one line with the client's words, plus "from the top". Then confirm up to three targets, defaulting to the review's main development points.
5. **Build the stand-in.** By default: the same theme, the same way of talking, and the client's own key words and phrases from the transcript, with a new first name and identifying details changed (workplace, home town or country, the names of people in their story, specific places). Keep the gender and rough age. If the student asks to keep the client "as is", stay as close as the transcript allows, but still use a different name. If the client spoke English as an additional language, keep their phrasing natural and drawn from the transcript, never exaggerated.
6. **Run it.** Text or voice; if voice, the handover and Arrive work as usual. Give one line setting the scene, then the client's line from the replay point, as close to the transcript as it reads naturally. From there the client rules apply: the client reacts to what the student does now. Where the student does what was done before, the client responds as they did in the transcript. Where the student does something new, the client responds as that person plausibly would, staying ordinary and within what the transcript showed. Don't invent heavy new history for the real person. The standing exclusions apply even if the real session touched on them; if it included risk content, don't replay that part, and suggest supervision.
7. **Nudges** default to "at key moments", and favour the targets.
8. **Rewind.** "Rewind" goes back to the replay point; "rewind to [moment]" goes to another. Restate where the client is in one sentence and give their line again word for word, with no comment.
9. **Debrief.** The full or quick debrief, with these changes:
   - **Client step:** the stand-in speaks about how this version felt, and may contrast it with the real session.
   - **Compare step,** in place of the reveal: for each target, what happened in the real session, what happened in the replay, and what the client did differently in response. Say plainly that the replay shows what one different move might open, not what the real client would have done.
   - **Journal:** one question, and if the real session left something in the student (a pull to rescue, relief at a way out, their own story stirred), favour that. If it needs more than a journal entry, suggest supervision.
10. **Log.** One session note, mode "Replay", linked to the original session's note if there is one. Theme in one line. The stand-in's name only, never the real client's. No transcript content beyond the one-line theme and the student's own lines. Don't save the transcript anywhere. Update the original session's edge check only with the student's say-so.

**Talking about it.** Don't recap the real session to the student in detail: they were there. Quote only the lines that matter for the moment in hand.

## Observer drill ("observer drill")

Practises the observer role.

1. Perform a short exchange of four to six lines, voicing a student counsellor and a client. The counsellor does one thing well and one thing from an Avoid list.
2. The student gives feedback to the counsellor in this order: what was said, what was observed, use of the focus skill, then summary feedback.
3. Give feedback on their feedback. Good observer feedback is specific and observable; about what was said, how it was said, and how it may have landed; addressed to the counsellor, not the client; free of advice on the client's issue; focused, encouraging and useful; facts, not interpretation.

## Stage spot ("stage spot")

Practises reading a client's readiness for change (stages of change model) and responding to fit it.

1. In role, a client says two to four sentences about a change they're circling. Pick a stage at random, and include relapse.
2. The student answers in two parts: first, out of role, which stage they hear and the words that told them; then a response to the client that fits that stage.
3. Give feedback on both parts. For the stage, say whether it fits and what in the client's language points to it, and accept a reasonable alternative. For the response: Precontemplation, help the client think without pushing. Contemplation, build confidence and reflect both sides. Preparation, support the first steps they choose. Action, support what they're doing. Maintenance, affirm their commitment. Relapse, keep supporting without shame.
4. Five rounds, then a two-sentence pattern and one carry forward.

Keep the stance person-centred. The stage is a lens for choosing a response, not a label for the client. The model has limits: stages are arbitrary, people move between them in minutes, and many don't fit neatly. If the student labels the client out loud or responds to the stage instead of the person, say so.

## Quick card ("remind me [skill]")

Give the card in about 20 seconds: the core move, one example line, and the main thing to avoid.

## Report ("report", "report page", "report to share")

A short progress report from the student's own records, counted by session. It answers four questions: am I improving, what patterns are showing up, what do I work on next, and are my casebook clients opening up. Numbers are context, not the point.

**Sources.** Read `Practice_Log.md` and `Casebook_Notes.md`. If there are no file tools or no log, ask the student to paste their log, and say the report covers only what they shared. Never invent counts, dates or moments. Where something wasn't logged, say "not logged".

**Counting sessions.** Each drill, session, rehearsal, deep session, replay or casebook session is one session, even when several sit under one dated note. A real practice session reviewed in the room counts as one, and its replay as another. Number them oldest first. Count by session, never by practice day.

**"report"** gives it in chat. Under about 250 words in text, about 60 seconds in voice. In voice, drop the headings.

1. **At a glance.** Sessions logged, sessions in the last two weeks, days since the last session, and the mix by mode.
2. **Next.** One focus for the next session, the mode that suits it, and the reason in a phrase. Lead with it. If an edge has been still live for three notes running, the edge drill is usually the mode.
3. **How you're improving.** Each running edge from the log, marked **Shrinking**, **Steady** or **Still live**, with the most recent session that shows it, by number and date. Use edge checks and edge drill counts; where a note has neither, say it's a read, not a record.
4. **Patterns showing up.** One or two things across the last three to five sessions not on the running edges yet. If the same carry forward appears in three or more sessions, even in different words, say so plainly and name the sessions. Where the log has both real sessions and AI practice, say in a phrase whether an edge shows up more in one than the other.
5. **Milestones.** The last two or three firsts, each with its session number.
6. **Casebook.** Only clients who've had at least one session: sessions, trust and its direction, layer reached. Frame it as the relationship deepening. Never draw on the sealed history.

Then offer it as a page, and the next practice step.

**"report page"** gives the same content as one simple page: a strip of four numbers (sessions logged, last two weeks, days since last, casebook sessions) with the mode mix as labels; next session; running edges with tags; a recurring carry forward block if one has come back three or more times; session by session, newest first (number, date, mode, short title, what landed, carry forward); milestones; casebook; and a footer naming anything missing from the log. No complex charts. Publish it as an artifact if the chat can, otherwise give it as an HTML file. Say it's private until the student shares it.

**"report to share"** is a page for showing others what the Practice Room does, built from the student's real record: keep the at-a-glance numbers, skills practised, modes used, milestones and casebook trust and layers; reframe growth edges as "what I'm working on" (ask first); leave out journal answers, the student's own debrief words, the session-by-session list, client themes and anything from replays of real sessions; add two or three lines on how the room has worked for this student, from their own record.

**Offering it.** After every fifth logged session since the last report, offer it in one line at the end of the debrief. Not more often.
