# Skill cards

One card per skill. "Body" lines are for in-person practice and can't be tested in voice.

## Opening and goal setting

- **Summary.** Building the first connection, creating psychological safety, and agreeing a shared focus.
- **Set up safety.** Greet warmly and give the client a moment to settle. Use a simple grounding line about pace and choice. Give permission to pause. Name that this is a practice space and that it may feel different. Name the time and the structure.
- **Core moves.** Ask an open question to find the focus. Agree what would make the session feel useful. Summarise the agreed focus and check it.
- **Sounds like.** "How are you arriving today?" "We've got ten minutes, and you can share as much or as little as you like. What would feel useful to look at?" "So you'd like to look at ... Have I got that right?"
- **Think about.** How are my words and presence helping the client feel safe? Is there a clear, simple intention?
- **Notice in the client.** Comfort, openness, nervousness. Normalise nerves gently.
- **If the client drifts.** Reflect, then re-anchor: come back to what they hoped to explore and check it still feels right.
- **Avoid.** Over-directing or rushing. Seeking emotional depth too early. Stacking several opening questions; one clean invitation is enough. Telling a reluctant client they "need to make it a choice"; reflect the reluctance instead. Apologising for a direct question before the client has had a chance to answer it ("sorry, that's a bit strong"); ask it, then watch.
- **Body.** Undivided attention, steady eye contact, still and open posture.
- **Observer checks.** Was time named? Did the client choose the focus? Was it summarised and checked?

## Listening and attending

- **Summary.** Undivided attention, non-verbal engagement and emotional attunement.
- **Set up safety.** Ground yourself before you begin and clear distractions.
- **Core moves.** Listen for the emotional tone beneath the words. Match your tone to the feeling expressed. Validate briefly. Keep interruptions minimal and leave silences alone.
- **Sounds like.** "That makes a lot of sense." "I can hear how much that got to you." Plus brief encouragers and space.
- **Think about.** Am I offering full presence, or preparing my next line? Am I emotionally available to what's being shared?
- **Notice in the client.** Moments of feeling heard, such as a sigh, softening or going deeper. If they seem unsure, pause and check in about how sharing is going.
- **If the client drifts.** Stay with the emotional tone, not just the content, and reflect the feeling back.
- **Avoid.** Thinking ahead to your next question. Giving advice. Filling silence. A shocked or disbelieving tone, which can tell the client they're odd or unacceptable.
- **Body.** Soft eye contact, open stillness, gentle nods, calm breathing. Not overly intense eye contact.
- **Observer checks.** Proportion of client talk to counsellor talk. Were silences left alone? Were validations matched to the feeling actually expressed?

## Paraphrasing, restating and summarising

- **Summary.** Helping the client feel heard and clearer by echoing and distilling their words.
- **Core moves.** Paraphrase the message in your own words and check it. Restate a key word to slow the pace and show attention. Summarise themes after a longer stretch. Paraphrasing tracks content; feeling belongs to reflection.
- **Sounds like.** Paraphrase: "So you felt stuck, and unsure how to respond. Is that right?" Restate: "Stuck." Summary: "So this week's had the work pressure, the lack of sleep, and not much room for you. Is that your sense of it?"
- **Check-outs.** Open check-outs ("What have I missed?", "How close is that?") tend to bring corrections and new material. Closed ones ("Is that right?") tend to get a flat yes. Prefer open ones, and watch for "Yes, but...", which means go back to reflecting what they actually said.
- **Repair when you miss.** Thank them for correcting you and try again, warmly and without defensiveness.
- **Think about.** What are the core ideas and feelings here? Am I reflecting, or just repeating?
- **Notice in the client.** Do they go deeper, correct you, or become more focused? Puzzled or flat responses mean the reflection probably missed.
- **If the client drifts.** Summarise and ask where they'd like to go from here.
- **Avoid.** Word-for-word repetition with no synthesis. Adding your own meaning. Using summaries to control the pace rather than clarify. Summaries that slip into your own analysis ("I imagine there are correlations"); if the client already linked the threads, reflect that they did.
- **Observer checks.** Main idea caught? Anything missed or misread? Checked back? Shorter than the original?

## Open questions

- **Summary.** Purposeful prompts that invite exploration. Closed questions are for specific facts.
- **Core moves.** Reflect before asking. Ask one at a time. Know what gap you're inviting the client to explore. Notice your intention: are you researching, being rhetorical, or challenging? Ask for the client's process, not your own curiosity. Phenomenological questions ask what and how about experience as it is now: "What's that like, right now?"
- **Sounds like.** "What's that like for you?" "What's most alive in that for you right now?"
- **Avoid.** Stacked questions. Leading questions ("Don't you think...?", "Have you tried...?"). Strings of questions with no reflection between them. "Why" questions, which ask the client to justify and put them on the defensive. Questions driven by interest in the story. Asking a question where offering a reflection would do more. A pet question used as a reflex after reflections ("How does that sit?"). Saying your doubt about the question out loud; keep it inside and let the client's answer tell you.
- **Observer checks.** One at a time? Preceded by a reflection? Serving the client's exploration?

## Reflection stems

- **Summary.** The openers that carry a reflection. Having a range on tap stops one stem becoming a reflex, and lets the student match the opener to the rung.
- **Three families:**
  - **Observation** (rung 1): "I can see...", "I notice...", "You went quiet just then." Names what's in front of you without interpreting. "I can see your eyes have filled up" is observation; "I can see you're upset" is a guess dressed as one.
  - **Tentative feeling** (rung 4): "That sounds like...", "It seems like...", "There's something like..." Offers a read the client can correct.
  - **Felt sense and meaning** (rungs 4 to 7): "It's as if...", "Part of you...", "I wonder if..." Reaches further, toward advanced empathy and meaning.
- **"I wonder what" versus "I wonder if".** "I wonder what..." is still a question underneath, which suits openings, where the client should lead. "I wonder if..." offers your read, which suits the reach later in a session.
- **The main trap.** A good stem with a vague ending. "That sounds like a strong experience" is safe and tells the client nothing new. The stem earns its place when the word after it is specific: "That sounds like it really rattled you."
- **Think about.** Which stem do I use by default? Which family do I rarely reach for?
- **Avoid.** One stem every time. Stacking stems ("I wonder if it sounds like maybe..."). Using a stem to soften a question you should have turned into a reflection.
- **Observer checks.** Did the stem fit the rung? Was the word after it specific? Any stem repeated as a reflex?

## Use of silence

- **Summary.** Silence hands the client control of content, pace and direction. The counsellor listens to silences as well as words, and lets them do some of the work.
- **Core moves.** After the client finishes, wait a breath or two before responding. When they go quiet mid-thought, don't rescue them. When a silence runs long and the client seems stuck rather than reflective, offer something gentle and minimal.
- **Sounds like.** Nothing, then: "Take your time." Or, after a long pause: "What's coming up for you in the quiet?"
- **Think about.** Whose discomfort am I responding to when I break a silence: the client's or mine?
- **Notice in the client.** The difference between a reflective silence (looking inward, slowing down) and a stuck or anxious one (looking for you to help).
- **Avoid.** Filling every gap. Jumping in with a question the moment the client stops. Letting a stuck, uncomfortable silence run on with no contact.
- **Voice drill note.** In drills, the client sometimes stops mid-thought. The student practises leaving it, or answering with a minimal encourager, instead of asking a new question.
- **Observer checks.** Were pauses left alone? Did the client continue on their own after a silence? Was any silence broken too early?

## Focusing

- **Summary.** When a client raises several issues, focusing helps them choose what matters most and set the rest aside for now. It can happen at any point when the session is spreading.
- **Core moves.** Summarise the threads you've heard. Ask the client which one feels most important right now. Follow their choice, not yours.
- **Sounds like.** "There's the job, your sister, and not sleeping. Which of those feels most important to stay with today?"
- **Think about.** Am I helping them choose, or choosing for them because one thread interests me more?
- **Notice in the client.** Energy: which thread they return to, or speak about with more feeling.
- **Avoid.** Picking the focus yourself. Narrowing too early. Treating the threads they didn't pick as closed for good.
- **Observer checks.** Were the threads named fairly? Did the client make the choice?

## Reflecting feeling (empathic communication and deep listening)

- **Summary.** Listening beyond content to the client's emotional world, responding with felt understanding, and staying attuned.
- **Core moves.** Let the client lead and follow rather than direct. Name the emotion beneath the words gently. Validate the experience. Match emotional intensity and tone without mimicking. Use brief, resonant reflections. Let silence do some of the work. Three moves get extra practice here:
  - **Hear the thought, find the feeling.** Feelings are usually one word (hurt, angry, flat). "I feel that..." or "I feel like..." followed by a string of words is usually a thought. Reflect the feeling underneath it. "I feel like nobody listens to me at work" might be met with "Sounds lonely" or "You're fed up," depending on tone.
  - **Feeling-only reflections.** When the client keeps escaping into story or detail, leave the content out so there's no route back into it: "You're really hurting."
  - **Reflect what you observe.** Name something you noticed, without interpreting it: "Your voice dropped when you mentioned your sister." "You laughed just then." Then leave it with the client.
- **Intensity ladder.** Pitch the word to the strength the client has shown: annoyed, angry, furious; uneasy, anxious, terrified; disappointed, sad, devastated. Overshooting and undershooting both leave the client unmet.
- **Sounds like.** "There's a lot of sadness under that." "It sounds like this matters to you deeply." "That hit hard." "You're really hurting." "You went quiet just then."
- **Think about.** Am I attuning to the feeling behind the words? Can I stay present without analysing or fixing?
- **Notice in the client.** Signs of feeling deeply heard, emotional shifts, points of resistance.
- **If the client drifts.** Use an empathic reflection to re-centre on what feels most alive for them.
- **Avoid.** Advice or interpretation. Overly positive or dismissive language ("At least...", "It'll be okay"). Talking more than listening. The tack-on: a good reflection followed straight away by a question that lifts the client out of the feeling. Presumptive frames ("That must have felt...") where "Sounds like..." leaves room to be corrected. Asking for the feeling ("How did you feel?") when the moment calls for offering one. Long reassurance just as the client goes vulnerable. Front-loaded reassurance or praise before a reflection. Generalising instead of using the client's specific words. Jumping to coping ("How are you managing that?") before the feeling has been met. "If I were you, I'd feel..." as a default stem. Turning an observation into an interpretation ("You laughed, so you must be uncomfortable with it").
- **Scope.** Inviting the client to notice where a feeling sits in the body, and staying with it, is in scope; staying with it is the intervention. Working with the sensation more actively belongs with Re-experiencing, and specialist somatic methods sit outside this practice room. Students with a somatic background should ask, each time: is this body invitation for the client, or is it how I step back when the feeling or the question gets hot? If the client has just asked something direct, meet that first.
- **Body.** Soft tone, leaning slightly forward, nodding.
- **Observer checks.** Feeling named specifically and tentatively, at the right intensity? When the client said "I feel that...", did the counsellor find the feeling underneath? Was a feeling-only reflection used when the client escaped into story? Was an audible shift noticed? Did the client go further afterwards?

## Advanced empathy

- **Summary.** Voicing what the client hasn't quite said yet: the feeling or meaning just under their words. It's a reach, so it only lands when it's built on accurate basic reflection first.
- **Core moves.** Listen for what the client's words are doing (performing, minimising, judging themselves). Reach for the thing underneath that. Say it tentatively, in one sentence or even one word, in the client's language. Then stop.
- **Where to look.** Self-judgment straight after a feeling ("which makes me a terrible person") usually hides fear about what the feeling means. Minimising ("first-world problems") usually hides something hollow or disappointing. A reaction at an odd moment usually means something held back is leaking out. Fault-finding with something good usually points to the good thing itself being unfamiliar or unsettling.
- **Darker material.** Being abrupt is about how you reach, not whether you reach. Point at the territory without naming the loaded word, and let the client fill it in.
- **Sounds like.** "I wonder if that flatness actually scared you a little." "Emptiness." "Sometimes safe and kind can still feel uncomfortable." "Something you haven't let yourself feel finding a way out."
- **One-word reflections.** Land hardest when you're confident they're accurate. If less sure, soften: "Something like emptiness?"
- **Think about.** Am I reaching inward, toward what the client feels about themselves, or outward toward the story and other people?
- **Notice in the client.** A pause, a word repeated back ("relentless... yeah, that's the word"), a voice dropping. That's the reach landing. Leave it alone.
- **Avoid.** Long, tangled reaches. Turning the reach into a question. Naming the loaded word flat ("you resent him"). Reassurance or praise wrapped around the reach. Following a good reach with "how does that sit?".
- **Observer checks.** One clean sentence? Tentative? Aimed at what was underneath? Did the counsellor stop afterwards? Did the client go further?

## Therapeutic presence (after Geller)

- **Summary.** A calm, grounded, emotionally available presence that helps the client feel safe, seen and connected: fully engaged, grounded and attuned in the present moment. Mindfulness prepares it; empathy arises from it.
- **Set up safety.** Start with a pause or breath to settle yourself, or the full Arrive sequence. Offer a warm, steady line about going at their pace.
- **Core moves.** Focus fully on the client, not on outcomes or what comes next. Stay connected to your body and breath. Offer brief, heartfelt reflections. Let silence be. Let emotion arise without trying to soothe it away. Contain without controlling.
- **Reaches.** Small exits from presence, where something in the room gets uncomfortable and a habit fills the gap: filling a silence with a question, reassurance or teaching, a clever line, seeking approval, reaching for root cause early, or voicing your own doubt out loud. The skill isn't never reaching. It's noticing the drift and coming back. The edge drill practises a student's own reaches directly.
- **Inside voice.** Doubts and hypotheses will come up ("is this too much?", "maybe she wants to give up"). Keep them inside. Turn the doubt into watching: a few seconds on the client's face, body or voice, then respond to what you see. Their response answers the doubt better than saying it would.
- **Barriers.** Fatigue, triggers, boredom, and trying too hard to do it right.
- **Online.** More of your presence travels through voice: tone, pacing, breath, and more frequent check-ins.
- **Sounds like.** "Take your time. I'm right here." "That sounds like it really stayed with you." Few words, well timed.
- **Think about.** Am I grounded in my body and breath? How do I know when I've left, and what brings me back?
- **Notice in the client.** Softening and deepening. If they seem distant, offer presence rather than a question.
- **If the client drifts.** Re-anchor with presence: slow things down and ask what's standing out most right now.
- **Avoid.** Rushing, analysing or directing. Leaning on technique instead of presence. Trying to manage discomfort away.
- **Body.** Softened shoulders, steady breath, open and still.
- **Observer checks.** In voice: unhurried pace, fewer and better-timed responses, silence left alone, no rush to reassure. Any reaches, and did the student come back?

## Immediacy

- **Summary.** Naming what's happening in the present moment, emotionally or between counsellor and client, so the relationship itself becomes part of the work.
- **Set up safety.** Name that you might check in about how it feels to be here together. Invite the client to say if something doesn't land. Use soft, curious language.
- **Core moves.** Notice and name a present-moment shift. Share your own experience briefly and gently, if relevant, then hand it back to the client. Ask about the space between you. When a pattern the client has described shows up live (the critic, the apology, the shrinking), name it as it happens.
- **Sounds like.** "I noticed you paused just then. What's happening for you right now?" "What's it like talking about this here, with me?" "As I listen, I'm feeling quite moved. How is this for you to talk about?" "You've apologised a couple of times now for taking up space here." "There's that voice again, right now."
- **Think about.** Am I grounded in my own state? Am I inviting connection without pressure?
- **Notice in the client.** Shifts in tone or energy, avoidance, sudden changes of subject. A pattern they've described happening live in the room.
- **If the client drifts.** Use immediacy to re-ground: name that something seemed to shift, or check you're still on what matters most.
- **Avoid.** Interpretations or assumptions ("You're angry with me"). Overuse, which makes it feel intrusive or clinical. Ignoring discomfort. Softening the pattern when it shows up live ("it's a productive solution in a way") instead of naming it. Use it gently, with timing and choice.
- **Observer checks.** Grounded in something that just happened? Tentative? Focus handed back to the client?

## Culture and worldview

- **Summary.** Culture shapes how a client makes sense of what's happening: what counts as a problem, what caused it, what would help, and who gets a say. A reflection can match the client's words and still miss their frame. The stance is humility and curiosity about this person, not a checklist of facts about groups.
- **Core moves.** Hold your own values lightly and notice when one is shaping your response. Ask about the client's frame rather than supplying yours. Reflect within their frame. Notice power and privilege in the room. Where difference is present, name it gently if it seems to matter to the client, then hand it back.
- **Explanatory model questions** (adapted from Kleinman): "What do you call what's happening?" "What do you think has caused it?" "What do the people close to you make of it?" "What do you hope will help, and who would you usually turn to?" One at a time, in your own words, after a reflection.
- **Intersectionality.** A client is never one identity. Faith, gender, generation, class, sexuality and migration history interact, and the client decides which matter here.
- **Being judged.** When a client says they feel judged (for their accent, background or difference), reflect their experience rather than confirming or denying the facts: "You can see it in their faces, and it stings." Your own view of whether people judge, or of a group, is yours; keep it out.
- **Sounds like.** "Family comes first for you, and right now that's pulling against what you want." "What would it mean, in your family, to say no to this?" "I'm aware I might not see this the way you do. Tell me how it looks from where you stand."
- **Think about.** What did I assume before the client told me? Which of my values showed up in that response?
- **Notice in the client.** Polite agreement after a reflection, which can mean an imposed value rather than a landed one. Checking whether you'll understand. Relief when their frame is taken seriously.
- **Avoid.** Assuming a background from a name or voice. Treating the client as a spokesperson, or asking them to educate you. Pathologising collective values. Ignoring culture altogether, and the opposite, putting everything down to culture. Reassurance that you "totally understand".
- **Observer checks.** Did responses stay in the client's frame? Was the client's own explanation invited? Any of the counsellor's values carried in? How did the client respond?

## Rupture and repair

- **Summary.** Noticing when something you said has cooled the connection, and bringing the client back. Ruptures are normal. Repairing one well often deepens the relationship.
- **Signs of a rupture.** The client goes polite, agreeable or flat. Answers shorten. Self-deprecation appears. Or the client corrects you outright.
- **Core moves.** Name what you noticed, gently. Own your part briefly, without grovelling. Reframe the space as shared, while keeping any boundary that matters. Then return to the client.
- **Sounds like.** "I can see something closed a little just then. I think that came out flatter than I meant." "This is a space where we work on this together."
- **Think about.** Am I defending what I said, or staying with how it landed?
- **Avoid.** Pretending it didn't happen. Over-apologising, which puts the client in the position of reassuring you. Repeating the point that caused the rupture once the repair has landed.
- **Observer checks.** Was the withdrawal noticed? Was ownership brief and real? Did the client come back?

## "What do I do?" moments

- **Summary.** When a client asks directly for help, the choice isn't between advice and abandonment. Meet them in the question and keep the work shared.
- **Core moves.** Reflect the question and the feeling under it (often fear of facing something alone). Acknowledge it's a real question. Make clear the figuring-out happens together, while the choices stay theirs.
- **Sounds like.** "You're asking how a person even starts again after all this. That's a big question, and it's exactly what we can work out together."
- **Think about.** Are they asking for a plan, or asking not to be alone with it?
- **Avoid.** Handing over a plan. Stating the principle flat ("those are your own strategies, that's the point"). Deflecting the question into the body or a new question.
- **Observer checks.** Was the question met rather than sidestepped? Did the client stay engaged?

## Unpacking and triggers

- **Summary.** Opening a compressed experience layer by layer so the feeling, meaning and pattern inside it become visible. Five steps: presenting moment, sensory and body awareness, emotional awareness, associative meaning, integration. Body and feeling come before meaning.
- **Core moves.** Identify a live moment. Slow the pace. Tune into body and emotion. Reflect meanings. Support integration and choice.
- **Sequencing.** Order interventions to the client's readiness. Meet the present feeling before asking where it's familiar from. Patterns that surface from the client land; patterns named by the counsellor often feel like analysis.
- **Triggers.** A sudden, intense reaction in the present, often linked to the past. Validate first. Explore gently. Combine grounding (breath, senses) with reflection. Stay regulated yourself. Normalise it. End with the client's strengths.
- **Sounds like.** "Can we slow right down to the moment he said that?" "What did you notice in your body just then?" "And what was the feeling?" "Is there something familiar in it?"
- **Think about.** Am I following the client into the moment, or hunting for the origin?
- **Avoid.** Rushing the steps. Jumping to meaning or origin before the feeling has been met. Leading the client toward a memory. Too many questions in a row.
- **Observer checks.** Pace slow enough? Body and feeling before meaning? Did the pattern come from the client?

## Polarities

- **Summary.** Two parts of the client pulling different ways: wanting connection and fearing closeness, wanting change and fearing it. Not pathology. Inner conflict often signals change trying to emerge, and naming both sides reduces shame.
- **Core moves.** Reflect both sides fairly, joined with "and." Explore each side with curiosity rather than siding with one. Name the protective intention: the side that holds back is usually keeping something safe.
- **Sounds like.** "Part of you wants to say yes, and part of you is bracing." "That cautious part, what do you think it's trying to protect?"
- **Scope.** Chair work, voice dialogue and parts work are beyond this practice room. Stay with double-sided reflection, open exploration and protective intentions.
- **Think about.** Am I quietly on one side? Am I treating the "stuck" part as the problem?
- **Avoid.** "But," which cancels the first side. Recruiting the change side against the other. Resolving the conflict for the client.
- **Observer checks.** Both sides held fairly? Protective intention explored? Did the client soften toward the resisting part?

## Confronting

- **Summary.** Gently naming a discrepancy so the client can look at it. Supportive, not combative: you hold up two things side by side and let the client make sense of them.
- **Kinds of discrepancy.** Between two things the client said. Between what they say and what they do. Between their words and how they say them. Between what they want and what's happening.
- **Core moves.** Make sure the feeling has been met first. Name both sides fairly, using the client's own words, joined with "and" rather than "but". Then hand it back and leave space. Ask permission where it helps: "Can I share something I've noticed?"
- **Sounds like.** "You've said you're fine, and you've also mentioned you haven't slept properly in weeks." "You're smiling as you tell me this, and it sounds like it really hurt."
- **Timing.** Challenge belongs to the middle phase, once trust is strong. Keep it minimal in a first session.
- **Think about.** Am I curious about the discrepancy, or trying to catch the client out?
- **Notice in the client.** Do they pause and consider it, or defend? Defensiveness usually means it came too early or too hard. Go back to reflecting.
- **Avoid.** "But". Accusation or a gotcha tone. Confronting before there's trust. Resolving the discrepancy for them. Confronting a client in precontemplation about a problem they don't own yet.
- **Observer checks.** Feeling met first? Both sides named fairly, in the client's words? Joined with "and"? Did the client explore it or defend?

## Reflection of meaning

- **Summary.** Reflecting what an experience says about the client's values, purpose or sense of who they are. Feeling answers "what's this like for you?"; meaning answers "why does this matter so much to you?"
- **Core moves.** Listen for values words (should, matters, always been the one who, the kind of person). Reflect the value or identity under the feeling, tentatively. Leave the client room to put it in their own words.
- **Sounds like.** "It sounds like being the one people can rely on is a big part of who you are." "So it's not really about the house. It's about the life you built there." "What does it mean to you that he never apologised?"
- **Think about.** Am I reflecting their meaning, or the meaning I'd make of it?
- **Notice in the client.** A pause, a slower voice, "yeah, that's it". Meaning that lands often brings quiet.
- **Avoid.** Imposing a meaning or a moral. Reaching for meaning before the feeling has been met. Turning it into a lesson ("so what you've learned is..."). Reframing before the feeling has landed.
- **Observer checks.** Built on accurate feeling work? Tentative? The client's meaning rather than the counsellor's? Did the client go further?

## Working with ambivalence (motivational interviewing)

- **Summary.** Helping the client explore and resolve mixed feelings by drawing out their own motivations, not persuading, while affirming their autonomy. People are persuaded by what they hear themselves say.
- **Set up safety.** Welcome all feelings, including resistance. Affirm that the direction is their choice. Name that you're exploring mixed feelings and nothing has to change.
- **Core moves.** OARS: open questions, affirmations, reflections, summaries. Reflect both sides of the ambivalence. Listen for change talk (desire, ability, reasons, need) and reflect it. Leave sustain talk unargued. Affirm strengths and autonomy. The process runs engage, focus, evoke, plan; don't plan before you've evoked.
- **The righting reflex.** The helper's urge to fix or argue for the healthy choice. It backfires: the client defends the other side. Coaches tend to have a strong one.
- **Decisional balance.** Four boxes: benefits and costs of changing, benefits and costs of not changing. The box that matters most is usually the benefits of not changing. Use it lightly.
- **Rulers.** Importance and confidence, 0 to 10. The number is only a doorway; the follow-up does the work. Ask why it isn't lower ("what makes it a 5 and not a 2?"), which gets the client listing their own reasons for change. Asking why it isn't higher gets them listing obstacles. For confidence, ask what would move it up one point.
- **Amplified reflection.** When sustain talk is firm, reflect it back slightly overstated, in a plain, curious tone: "So there's nothing about it that worries you at all." The client usually softens it themselves and offers the other side. It only works without sarcasm; any edge in the voice makes them dig in.
- **Giving information (Ask-Offer-Ask).** When information would genuinely help, or the client asks for it: ask what they already know, ask permission, offer one short piece without telling them what to do with it, then ask what they make of it. The client stays the one deciding what it means.
- **Responding to discord.** Sustain talk is about the change; discord is friction between you and the client ("you sound like my wife", interrupting, going cool). Discord usually means you pushed, labelled or got ahead of them. Reflect it, emphasise autonomy ("it's your call"), shift focus away from the sticking point, or reframe the pressure they're under. If you overstepped, own it briefly (see Rupture and repair). Don't defend or explain your point.
- **Sounds like.** "Part of you wants things to change, and part of you isn't sure." "You mentioned wanting to feel more in control. Tell me more about that." "Whatever you decide, it's yours to decide." "What makes it a 6 and not a 3?"
- **Affirmation.** Affirm effort and the person, not performance. "It took something to look at that" builds safety. "Good self-awareness" rates the client.
- **Think about.** Am I letting the client lead, or quietly pushing? Whose voice is arguing for change in this session?
- **Notice in the client.** "I want to, but..." language. Stuckness. Shifts when they hear their own words reflected back. Sustain talk hardening, which usually means you pushed. Discord aimed at you rather than at the change.
- **If the client drifts.** Bring them back to the tension and check it still fits.
- **Avoid.** Advising, persuading or confronting. Oversimplifying the dilemma. Treating resistance as a lack of motivation. Arguing with sustain talk. "Why not higher?" after a ruler. Sarcastic or exaggerated amplified reflections. Unasked information, or more than two sentences of it. Defending yourself when discord shows up.
- **Stage note.** Ambivalence usually sounds like contemplation. A client in precontemplation needs something gentler: curiosity about how they see it. Practise with "stage spot" and "change talk drill".
- **Observer checks.** Both sides reflected fairly? Who argued for change, client or counsellor? Was change talk picked up? Any righting reflex? Did ruler follow-ups draw out change talk? Was information given with permission and handed back? Was discord met with reflection or autonomy rather than argument?

## Self-criticism and self-destructive processes

- **Summary.** Recognising and gently exploring the client's inner critic and patterns of self-sabotage, with compassion and curiosity, without reinforcing shame or trying to fix it. The critic is usually an internalised voice, and self-destructive beliefs ("I always fail") usually do a job: protection, familiarity, avoiding shame.
- **Set up safety.** Make clear the space is non-judgemental. Normalise harsh inner voices. Reinforce that they can share only what feels right.
- **Core moves.** Invite awareness of their self-talk and its tone. Reflect critical language with curiosity. Gently separate the client from the voice. Help them notice rather than evaluate. Affirm the part of them that wants something different. When the critic speaks in the room ("I know I'm just covering up"), name it gently as it happens rather than smoothing it over.
- **Shame.** It needs empathy, not logic, and presence, not reassurance. Stay with it; don't talk the client out of it.
- **Sounds like.** "What tone does that inner voice have?" "It sounds like there's a harsh voice in there. What's it like to hear it?" "Is there another part of you that wants something different?" "There's that voice again, right now."
- **Think about.** Am I attuned to the emotional impact of the critic? Am I offering warmth, not solutions?
- **Notice in the client.** Signs of shame (apologising, going quiet). Absolute language ("I always", "I'm just not good enough"). Moments of self-awareness or gentleness.
- **If the client drifts.** Return to what's underneath: wonder whether the voice just came in, or slow down and notice what happened.
- **Avoid.** Arguing with the critic ("But you're not like that!", "I'm understanding you perfectly right now"). Reassurance at a shame moment. Reframing or problem-solving before the feeling has been met. Minimising.
- **Client note.** Keep the critic ordinary and everyday (procrastination, perfectionism, comparing yourself to others). Never connect it to self-harm or risk.
- **Observer checks.** Curious rather than corrective? Did the counsellor slow down at shame signals, and stay rather than reassure? When the critic showed up live, was it noticed?

## Re-experiencing and sequencing

- **Summary.** Guiding the client to revisit a meaningful moment in more detail and staying close to it, moving through what came before, during and after.
- **Set up safety.** Set a slow pace with the client in charge. Invite rather than push. Keep them connected to the present, with no need to relive anything.
- **Core moves.** Invite description as if it's happening now: what they see, hear and feel. Ask about the body as they recall it. Use sequencing questions. Stay with the strongest part. Track and mirror the sequence back. See also Unpacking.
- **Sounds like.** "What was happening just before that?" "What happened next?" "So just before the overwhelm, there was a tightness in your chest. Is that right?"
- **Think about.** Am I slowing down enough for the client to feel into it?
- **Notice in the client.** Shifts in tone or pace. Signs of disconnection (flat, distracted, confused). If you see them, pause and ground.
- **If the client drifts.** Return gently to the moment, or check whether the image still feels important.
- **Avoid.** Rushing or problem-solving. Leading the client into a memory. Too many questions in a row.
- **Client note.** Choose a mild, everyday moment (an awkward conversation, a near-miss, a moment of pride). Nothing traumatic.
- **Observer checks.** Pace slow enough? Sequence tracked accurately? Did the client lead?

## Integration

- **Summary.** Bringing the skills together with confidence, clarity and intention, without trying to use everything. This is what an All skills session practises.
- **Core moves.** Start with grounding and contact. Let the session unfold. Be present, listen fully, reflect when it fits, use immediacy if something arises, and invite depth only when it feels natural.
- **Sounds like.** "What's felt most helpful or surprising today?" "What stands out for you, looking back on our conversation?"
- **Think about.** What comes naturally now? Where do I still hesitate?
- **Notice in the client.** Whether they're processing aloud. Give it room without rushing to tie it up.
- **If the client drifts.** Anchor gently to what mattered earlier, or name the time and ask what they'd like to be clear on before you finish.
- **Avoid.** Trying to impress or to be complete. Rushing to a solution. Technique over presence.
- **Observer checks.** Which skills appeared naturally? Where did technique replace contact?

## Ending a session

- **Summary.** Closing with care: signalling time, drawing threads together, and letting the client leave holding their own insight. A close isn't the same as stopping. A client who's just opened up needs gathering before they walk out the door. Across a course of therapy, ending is its own phase: review, consolidate, say goodbye, and hand the success back to the client.
- **Core moves.** Signal time a few minutes out. Summarise what was explored, or hand back the one phrase that carried it. If the client is raw, slow down and ground before closing. Close warmly. Don't open anything new.
- **Sounds like.** "We've got a couple of minutes left. Can I pull together what we've talked about?" "Stop waiting." (their phrase, handed back) "Something settled just then." "It took something to say all that." "We'll pick this up next week."
- **Praise or affirmation.** Affirm the person and their effort, not their performance. "I'm really glad you came" opens. "That's a great place to land" grades.
- **Think about.** Am I trusting what already landed, or reaching for one more thing?
- **Avoid.** Stopping abruptly. Opening a new thread at the end. Summaries that sound like conclusions or advice. The tack-on closing question straight after a good close. Pushing for action before the client has landed. Closing instructions dressed as reflections ("feel into how much you've shared").
- **Observer checks.** Time signalled? Thread gathered? Did the client leave with their own words? Did it feel finished, even if the issue wasn't?
